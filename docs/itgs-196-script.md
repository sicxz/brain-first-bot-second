> So today's question is simple: who is doing the thinking? You, or the bot?

## 6 · Same tool. Opposite results.

**On screen:** Study A (Bastani et al., *PNAS* 2025, ~1,000 high school math students): plain chatbot +48% practice, −17% exam once the AI was gone; tutor with guardrails +127% practice, exam harm largely gone. Study B (Kestin et al., *Scientific Reports* 2025, 194 Harvard physics students): 2×+ the learning gains, in less time. "The tool doesn't decide. How you use it decides."

**Say** (1 min 30 sec)
> Two studies. In the first, about a thousand high school students practiced math with GPT-4. With a plain chatbot, their practice scores jumped 48 percent. Then the AI was taken away for the exam, and they scored 17 percent worse than students who never had it. They let the bot do the thinking.
>
> Another group got a tutor version that gave hints, not answers. Their practice scores more than doubled, and the exam damage mostly disappeared.
>
> In the second study, Harvard students using an AI tutor built on good teaching learned more than twice as much as in a strong class, in less time.
>
> Same technology. Opposite results. The tool doesn't decide. How you use it decides.

## 7 · How learning actually works.

**On screen:** Study Cycle: Preview → Attend → Review → Study → Assess. Retrieval beats rereading (reread the notes ✗, quiz yourself ✓). The fluency trap: "I read the summary, I've got it" ✗, "I haven't tested myself yet" ✓.

**Say** (1 min 15 sec)
> Some of you heard Saundra McGuire speak on September 22. Her Study Cycle is preview, attend, review, study, assess.
>
> Two ideas sit underneath it. First, retrieval beats rereading. Pulling an idea out of your own head is what makes it stick, even when you get it wrong.
>
> Second, fluency fools you. A clean, smooth summary feels like knowing. An AI summary is the smoothest text you'll ever read. So if you catch yourself thinking "I read the summary, I've got it," translate that to "I haven't tested myself yet."

## 9 · Gemini Notebook on the Study Cycle.

**On screen:** Clickable ring. Each stage shows a notebook move and a prompt to try.
- **Preview:** play the audio overview on the walk to class; skim the mind map. Try: "Make a mind map of Chapter 4."
- **Attend:** bring one question the notebook couldn't answer. Try: "List three questions this chapter raises but doesn't answer."
- **Review:** quiz yourself right after class. Try: "Quiz me on Section 4.2, one question at a time. Wait for my answer."
- **Study:** flashcards, then explain a concept and ask what's missing. Try: "Here's my explanation of how to plan a degree path. What did I miss? Cite the chapter."
- **Assess:** practice quiz closed-book, then check citations on misses. Try: "Make a 10-question quiz on Chapter 4. After I finish, show which sections my misses came from."
- "Summaries live at the start of the cycle. Never at the end."

**Say** (1 min 15 sec)
> Here's how the notebook tools fit the Study Cycle. *(Click each stage.)*
>
> Preview is where summaries and audio overviews belong. They're a trailer for the chapter, not a replacement. Attend: bring a question the notebook couldn't answer.
>
> Review and Study are where quizzes and flashcards shine, because they make you retrieve. Assess: take a quiz without looking, then check the citations for what you missed.
>
> Notice where the summary sits: at the start, never at the end.

## 10 · Does the prompt think with you or for you?

**On screen:** Eight flip cards. Read one, ask the room, tap to reveal.

| With you | For you | Depends |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Quiz me on Section 4.2, one question at a time. Wait for my answer." | "Summarize Chapter 4 so I don't have to read it." | "Make me flashcards for Chapter 4." Great if you quiz yourself; useless if you only read them. |
| "Here's my explanation of how to plan a degree path. What did I miss? Cite the chapter." | "Write my journal entry about time management." | "Which idea in this chapter do first-years usually misunderstand?" Useful as a pointer; you still have to test it. |
| "Ask me three questions that connect this chapter to my major." | "Give me the answers to the quiz." | |

**Say** (1 min 30 sec)
> You don't need to be a prompt expert. You need a few good sentences. Let's poll. *(Read a card, ask "with or for?", then tap to reveal.)*
>
> "With you" prompts put you to work: the notebook asks, waits and critiques. "For you" prompts hand the work to the bot. My favorite: write your own explanation first, then ask what you missed, with a citation.
>
> The yellow ones depend on you. Flashcards only help if you actually quiz yourself.

## 11 · Try it: the 20-minute Notebook Sprint.

**On screen:** Ch. 4, with a built-in timer that chimes between steps.
- **0:00 Brain dump (3 min):** screens down. List three things you remember from Ch. 4 and one question.
- **0:03 Build (3 min):** new notebook, add the Ch. 4 PDF from Canvas.
- **0:06 Quiz (6 min):** "Quiz me with 8 questions, one at a time."
- **0:12 Receipts (4 min):** pick two answers and click the citations. Does the page really say that?
- **0:16 Pair share (4 min):** What did your brain dump miss? What did the notebook miss?
- **Exit line for your journal:** "AI helped me ___. It didn't help with ___."

**Say** (1 min 30 sec)
> Here's a routine for Chapter 4, Planning Your Academic Pathways. Twenty minutes.
>
> Start with your screen down and write what you remember. That's "brain first." Then build a notebook and let it quiz you.
>
> Then check the receipts: click two citations and see whether the page really says that. Finish with a partner: what did you miss, and what did the notebook miss? The exit line goes right into your journal.

*(Optional: start the timer and run it now.)*
